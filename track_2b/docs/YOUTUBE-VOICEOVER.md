## Script

**0:00 to 0:36: who we are, what you are about to see (about 95 words)**
*Picture: the intro animation, the title screen, the editor opening (clip seconds 0 to 17).*
Hi, we are team one-N-E, and this is GemeindeSim, our project for Hack Apertus 2026, Track 2B. In Switzerland a vote is decided by a written text, and that text can be wrong or misread. In 2019 a federal vote was even annulled because the booklet said eighty thousand couples were affected when the real number was four hundred and fifty-four thousand. GemeindeSim reads a voting text and lets a small fictional town react to it, so that you can see who understands what, before the text is printed. This video is one real, uncut run.

**0:36 to 1:32: the setup (about 125 words)**
*Picture: the text pasted, 25 residents, 5 rounds, Run, the setup screen "Linden, day 1, spring" (clip seconds 17 to 92).*
We paste a fictional municipal vote, in German and in French: the town of Linden wants to raise its tax multiplier from one hundred eighteen to one hundred twenty-four percent and build a school for four point eight million francs. The text also contains the referendum committee's arguments, because a Swiss booklet always prints the minority view. We ask for twenty-five residents and five rounds, and press run. Everything runs on the CSCS inference API, with Apertus one-point-five, seventy billion parameters, Switzerland's open model. While the setup runs, our code draws the residents: name, job, income, political leaning, and German or French. Apertus writes each person's background and the best argument for and against, in their own language. No real person's data is used.

**1:32 to 2:43: the town (about 155 words)**
*Picture: the map, speech bubbles, the event log; zoom on a citation chip in the log (clip seconds 92 to 163).*
Here is the town. Each resident walks around, talks to a neighbour, and the speech bubbles appear on the map. On the left is the event log, with the full text of every line. Notice three things. First, every resident speaks in their own language, German or French. Second, the little numbers in square brackets are citations: each one points to a passage of the voting text that the resident used, and our code checks that the passage really exists, because before we added that check, none of our first one hundred fifty-nine citations pointed at a real passage. Third, when a resident quotes a cost for their own household, such as one hundred forty-four francs for a low income, that figure was calculated by code, by scaling the worked example in the text, which says two hundred forty francs at eighty thousand, to the resident's income. The model did not do that sum.

**2:43 to 4:37: the numbers on the screen (about 240 words)**
*Picture: the run-metrics panel opened (clip second 163), the stance poll in the corner; the influence graph opens at about 277 (clip seconds 163 to 277).*
Bottom right is the run-metrics panel, and it is live. After about two and a half minutes the run has used about two hundred fifty thousand tokens in and sixty-five thousand out, around twenty-one percent of the prompt tokens came from the cache, the mean answer takes about nine seconds, and the app's adaptive limiter has opened up to thirty-two requests in flight, with no retries and no errors. The largest prompt uses one percent of the model's two hundred sixty-two thousand token window, so we never need to compact context. Above it is the stance poll: how many residents are for, undecided or against. This is the most important design decision of the project. When we asked Apertus directly for a stance, it said yes for all fifteen residents, even with no persona at all. On fifty-four real Swiss federal votes the simulated voters were thirty-four points too favourable, and the smaller model followed an official recommendation ninety-eight percent of the time. So the model never reports a stance. Our code computes it from the household cost, the resident's political leaning, and the model's judgement of whether the measure helps or hurts that household, and moves it only through conversations, with a standard opinion-dynamics rule. The poll says, in small print, that it is computed, not predicted. In a moment you will see the social graph: who spoke to whom, and how much influence each conversation had.

**4:37 to 7:37: while the town plays (about 275 words)**
*Picture: the map and the log run on; you can speed this stretch up (clip seconds 277 to 457).*
While the rounds play, here is what happens in each one. For every resident, our code retrieves the relevant passages from the text, builds the prompt, and asks Apertus for one answer in JSON: a few events such as chat, move or mood change. Before anything reaches the screen, code checks it: every citation must exist, every number must appear in the text or in the calculator, and the spelling is made Swiss, with no German eszett. Then the opinion dynamics run: a conversation changes a stance only if the two residents are close enough, and only residents who actually talked can move. We measured a lot while building this, more than twenty thousand logged calls in thirty-two experiments, and the results shaped the design. Apertus is a very good speaker: every line came in the right language. It is a poor decider. It also defines the Steuerfuss wrongly and does the percentage sum wrong, which is why the arithmetic is in code. It cannot call two tools at once, and a temperature of zero is not reproducible, so we treat every run as a draw and show ranges. The fixes you see here were measured on identical seeds: valid citations from zero to one hundred percent, reports that invent an outcome from twelve of thirty to none, and residents whose words match their stance from about half to about ninety percent. Please remember what this is and is not. It is a small fictional town, a what-if tool and a pre-test for the wording of a text. It is not a poll and not a vote predictor, and we say so in the report itself.

**7:37 to 8:31: the report (about 145 words)**
*Picture: the run completes; the report modal; scroll to the end (clip seconds 457 to 511).*
The run is complete and the report opens. It is written in the residents' majority language, here German. The headline and summary say who is affected and why, and the summary ends with the sentence that the report gives no voting recommendation. Under it, the stance line: twelve for, eight undecided, five against at the start, thirteen, seven and five at the end, with the reminder that this is a small fictional town, not a forecast. Then the livelihood impact, with the two hundred forty francs from the text's worked example, and a row of figures: twenty-five residents, the dominant mood hopeful at fifty-two percent, thirty-six percent under strain. Scrolling down, the final sentiment, the signals during the run, the top impacts, positive for the building sector, negative for household budgets, and the notable events. A human reads this and decides what to change in the text.

**8:31 to 9:20: talking to a resident (about 125 words)**
*Picture: the resident profile and the chat, questions 1 and 2 (clip seconds 514 to 562; stop before the third question).*
We can also talk to any resident. This is Lisa Steiner, a carpenter in a small building firm, with a low income and a strongly progressive leaning. We ask in German how much more tax her household would pay, and she answers with the calculated figure, one hundred forty-four francs, with an English translation underneath. We ask what the proposal says about the school credit, and her answer cites the passage it comes from, with the credit of four point eight million francs. If the text does not contain an answer, she says she does not know, and she never invents a figure.

**9:20 to 9:58: closing (about 90 words)**
*Picture: back on the map or the title card with the repository address (clip seconds 562 to 598).*
That is one complete run. The code is open source and the whole stack starts with one command, make run. Three environment variables point it at the CSCS API, the hackathon gateway or your own server, and the repository includes a recorded run you can replay and ready-to-paste scenarios, in German, French and English, including one text that is deliberately ambiguous. We tested everything on two hosted endpoints; we have not yet measured a local deployment, and the German and French still need a native speaker's review. Thank you for watching. We are team one-N-E.
