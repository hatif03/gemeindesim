# GemeindeSim: Verified Literature Review

Status: written 5 Oct 2026 for Hack Apertus, Track 2B.

## Purpose and how to read this

This review places GemeindeSim (a generative-agent town simulation for Swiss municipal votes and budgets, DE/FR, running on Apertus 1.5, with opinion updates done in Python rather than by the LLM) against the published evidence on LLM social simulation, LLM opinion dynamics, LLM political behaviour (especially Swiss), persona robustness, evaluation methodology, structured output, RAG grounding and cost.

Verification convention. Every entry below was checked by opening the arXiv, ACL, publisher or project page during this session and reading the abstract or key text. Entries tagged **[V]** were opened and the stated claim is on the page. Entries tagged **[S]** were seen only as a web-search result snippet (page not opened or blocked); they are used sparingly and flagged. Numbers are quoted only when they appeared on a page I read. Where I cite a number from the Apertus technical report, it comes from a local text extraction of the PDF I downloaded, not from the abstract page.

Three headline conclusions:

1. The literature supports GemeindeSim's central architectural decision (LLM writes language, code owns opinion state), because LLM-only opinion dynamics show truth/consensus bias, sycophancy and homogenisation (Section 2).
2. The literature does not support treating any such simulation as predictive of real votes without validation; the one directly relevant Swiss study found that model "politics" depends on the measuring instrument (Section 3).
3. The biggest cheap wins are measurement, not new machinery: a per-resident stance variable, population-level diversity metrics, a persona/language perturbation audit, and citation/numeral faithfulness metrics (Section 7).

## 1. LLM agent social simulation and its validity

### 1.1 Park et al. 2023, Generative Agents [V]
Park, J.S., O'Brien, J.C., Cai, C.J., Morris, M.R., Liang, P., Bernstein, M.S. (2023). *Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior.* arXiv:2304.03442 (published at UIST 2023; the ACM link 10.1145/3586183.3606763 appeared in search results, not opened). https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442
Claim: agents with a natural-language memory stream, reflection and retrieval-driven planning produce believable individual and emergent behaviour (the abstract's example is a Valentine's Day party organised from one seed suggestion). The abstract describes the three components (memory, reflection/synthesis, dynamic retrieval for planning). A search-result summary says ablations showed each component mattered for interview-task believability; I did not verify that on the page.
**Implication for GemeindeSim:** the memory/retrieve/reflect/plan/act loop is the standard baseline, but the paper's evidence is believability, not validity against real populations (see 1.7). Describe our agents as "plausible", not "representative". Keep a small ablation (reflection on/off) in our own eval so we can say what reflection contributes to stance stability.

### 1.2 Park et al. 2024, 1,052-person simulation [V]
Park, J.S. et al. (2024). *LLM Agents Grounded in Self-Reports Enable General-Purpose Simulation of Individuals* (the "1,000 people" paper). arXiv:2411.10109. https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109
Claim: agents conditioned on interview transcripts of 1,052 Americans reached 83%, 82% and 86% of participants' own two-week test-retest consistency on the tasks reported (per the abstract summary), outperformed demographics-only baselines by 12 percentage points, and reduced accuracy disparities across racial and ideological groups. Note the page says 1,052 participants, not 1,000.
**Implication:** the strongest evidence in the field says rich individual-level grounding beats thin demographic personas. GemeindeSim's personas (role, MBTI, a political-leaning field) are the "thin" kind. Even a 5-10 line life-and-values backstory per resident, ideally written from real Swiss survey-style attributes, is the literature-supported upgrade. Do not claim interview-grade fidelity.

### 1.3 Argyle et al. 2023, silicon samples [V]
Argyle, L.P., Busby, E.C., Fulda, N., Gubler, J.R., Rytting, C., Wingate, D. (2023). *Out of One, Many: Using Language Models to Simulate Human Samples.* Political Analysis 31(3):337-351 (arXiv:2209.06899, abstract page opened; journal citation from a search result listing). https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.06899
Claim: GPT-3 conditioned on socio-demographic backstories shows "algorithmic fidelity": its bias is "fine-grained and demographically correlated" rather than uniform, so it can emulate subgroup response patterns.
**Implication:** this is the optimistic citation for persona conditioning. It was US-only and GPT-3; the German/Swiss replications below (1.5, 3.4) are the more relevant evidence. Use it to justify the approach, then cite the critiques.

### 1.4 Santurkar et al. 2023, whose opinions [V]
Santurkar, S., Durmus, E., Ladhak, F., Lee, C., Liang, P., Hashimoto, T. (2023). *Whose Opinions Do Language Models Reflect?* arXiv:2303.17548. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17548
Claim: OpinionsQA (60 US demographic groups) shows substantial misalignment between LM opinions and those of US demographic groups, comparable in size to the partisan gap on climate change; steering prompts did not fix it; seniors (65+) and widowed people were especially poorly represented. (ICML 2023 venue is from memory, not on the page I read.)
**Implication:** our residents who are older or non-mainstream are the ones most likely to be mis-modelled. If we keep an "elderly resident" persona, do not let the LLM decide its stance; fix it in code from the persona.

### 1.5 Bisbee et al. 2024, synthetic survey data [V]
Bisbee, J., Clinton, J.D., Dorff, C., Kenkel, B., Larson, J.M. (2024). *Synthetic Replacements for Human Survey Data? The Perils of Large Language Models.* Political Analysis 32(4):401-416. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-analysis/article/synthetic-replacements-for-human-survey-data-the-perils-of-large-language-models/B92267DC26195C7F36E63EA04A47D2FE
Claim (ChatGPT 3.5 Turbo, ANES personas, feeling thermometers): averages roughly match, but variance is unrealistically low, 48% of regression coefficients differ significantly from ANES estimates and effect directions flip in 32% of problematic cases; the identical prompt gave different results in April and July 2023 after a model update; small persona wording changes shifted distributions.
**Implication:** three concrete risks for us: (a) low variance across residents (add a diversity metric), (b) sensitivity to persona wording (add a perturbation audit), (c) model-version drift (pin Apertus model id and log it with every run; the endpoint is hosted).

### 1.6 Wang, Morgenstern, Dickerson 2025, misportrayal and flattening [V]
Wang, A., Morgenstern, J., Dickerson, J.P. (2025). *Large language models that replace human participants can harmfully misportray and flatten identity groups.* Nature Machine Intelligence; arXiv:2402.01908. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01908
Claim: LLMs misportray and flatten demographic groups; shown empirically on 4 LLMs through human studies with 3,200 participants across 16 identities; identity prompts can essentialise identities; inference-time techniques reduce but do not remove the harms.
**Implication:** a MBTI plus "leaning: left" persona is exactly the kind of identity-keyed prompt this paper warns about. Frame personas as role-based and issue-based (tenant, shop owner, parent) rather than identity-based, and state in the report that residents are fictional archetypes, not models of real groups.

### 1.7 Hewitt et al. 2026, predicting social-science experiments [V, with caveat]
Hewitt, L., Ashokkumar, A., Ghezae, I., Willer, R. *Large language models can predict the results of social science experiments* (earlier preprint title: *Predicting results of social science experiments using large language models*). Stanford GSB publication page lists it in Nature, July 2026. https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/large-language-models-can-predict-results-social-science-experiments
Claim: on 70 pre-registered US survey experiments, GPT-4 simulated responses predicted treatment effects with accuracy comparable to pooled human forecasts, including for unpublished studies and open-weight models, with a systematic tendency to overestimate effect sizes; correlations were lower on a secondary set of 15 megastudies. Search snippets quote r = 0.85 and slightly different effect counts (469 vs 476); I cite only what the GSB page states and treat the exact numbers as unverified.
**Implication:** the positive result is about direction and relative size of treatment effects for US survey experiments, not about absolute vote shares. For GemeindeSim the transferable idea is to use the sim comparatively (policy A vs B, information condition 1 vs 2) rather than for absolute turnout/yes-share. Also note the overestimation of effect sizes: our dashboards should show relative shifts with uncertainty bands, not point predictions.

### 1.8 Counter-evidence on treatment effects and validation [V]
- Li, Z., Ji, F. (2026). *Statistical realism is not evidence that LLMs can estimate treatment effects in social science experiments.* arXiv:2604.02458. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02458 Across large cross-national experiments (nearly 80,000 participants) the correlation between statistical realism and treatment-effect accuracy was weak. **Implication:** matching marginal distributions (our future "distribution" metric) does not validate the dynamics; we need at least one effect-level check.
- Hullman, J., Broska, D., Sun, H., Shaw, A. (2026). *This human study did not involve human subjects: Validating LLM simulations as behavioral evidence.* arXiv:2602.15785. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15785 Distinguishes heuristic approaches (fine for exploratory work, no statistical guarantees) from statistical calibration against human data. **Implication:** label GemeindeSim as exploratory/heuristic; the calibration route needs real human data (e.g. smartvote or VOTO, Section 3).
- Anthis, J.R. et al. (2025). *LLM Social Simulations Are a Promising Research Method.* ICML 2025, arXiv:2504.02234. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02234 Argues current simulations are usable for pilot and exploratory studies and recommends iterative evaluation and context-rich prompting. **Implication:** supports our "pilot/what-if" positioning.

### 1.9 Platforms and critical reviews
- Concordia [V]: Vezhnevets, A.S., Leibo, J.Z. et al. (2023). *Generative agent-based modeling with actions grounded in physical, social, or digital space using Concordia.* arXiv:2312.03664. https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03664 A "Game Master" agent translates natural-language actions into environment effects. **Implication:** our Python-owned opinion update plays the Game Master role for the one state variable that matters; this is a defensible variant of the Concordia pattern.
- S3 [V]: Gao, C. et al. (2023, rev. 2025). *S3: Social-network Simulation System with Large Language Model-Empowered Agents.* arXiv:2307.14984. https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14984 Models emotion, attitude and interaction; claims validation against real social-network data with "promising accuracy". **Implication:** S3 treats attitude as an explicit model variable, which our gap (no per-resident stance) currently lacks.
- Y Social [V]: Rossetti, G., Stella, M., Cazabet, R. et al. (2024). *Y Social: an LLM-powered Social Media Digital Twin.* arXiv:2408.00818. https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00818 Open-source LLM social-media twin. **Implication:** useful as a reference implementation for logging and analysis hooks, not for civic deliberation.
- OASIS [V]: Yang, Z., Zhang, Z. et al. (2024, rev. 2025). *OASIS: Open Agent Social Interaction Simulations with One Million Agents.* arXiv:2411.11581. https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11581 Combines LLM and rule-based agents; replicates information spreading, group polarisation and herd effects; reports that larger populations gave "more enhanced group dynamics and more diverse and helpful agents' opinions". **Implication:** OASIS itself mixes LLM and rule-based agents, which supports our hybrid. The scale effect is a warning that 5-25 agents will show weaker, noisier group dynamics than published scale results; report variance across seeds.
- AgentSociety [V]: Piao, J. et al. (2025, rev. Apr 2026). *AgentSociety: Large-Scale Simulation of LLM-Driven Generative Agents Advances Understanding of Human Behaviors and Society.* arXiv:2502.08691. https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08691 Over 10,000 agents and 5 million interactions; five case studies including polarisation and universal basic income; claims alignment with real-world experimental results. **Implication:** the nearest policy-what-if precedent (UBI); we should cite it as prior art and note that our scale is three orders of magnitude smaller. (First-author name is from memory; the fetched pages did not list authors, so check before camera-ready.)
- Larooij and Törnberg [V]: Larooij, M., Törnberg, P. (2025). *Do Large Language Models Solve the Problems of Agent-Based Modeling? A Critical Review of Generative Social Simulations.* arXiv:2504.03274. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03274 Finds validation mostly rests on subjective believability, LLM integration likely exacerbates ABM problems (realism, compute, calibration), and LLM opacity hampers mechanism discovery. **Implication:** this is the strongest argument for our hybrid: because opinion change is an explicit equation, the mechanism is inspectable. Say so in the paper, and acknowledge the LLM-written dialogue remains a black box.
- Li, Y., Tao, D. (2026). *AI Agents Alone Are Not (Yet) Sufficient for Social Simulation.* arXiv:2603.00113. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00113 [V] "Role-playing plausibility does not imply faithful behavioural validity"; results depend on interaction protocol, scheduling and initial information distribution. **Implication:** log and report who talks to whom, in which order, and what each resident saw; these design choices are part of the result.
- Ye, J., Cao, L., Chen, D., Ferrara, E. (2026). *Stop Drawing Scientific Claims from LLM Social Simulations Without Robustness Audits.* arXiv:2605.18890. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18890 [V] Small perturbations in persona format and game framing shifted cooperation rates by up to 76 percentage points on some models and hardly at all on others; proposes the TRAILS audit across agent, interaction and system levels. **Implication:** direct template for the perturbation audit in Section 7 (rank 3).

## 2. LLM opinion dynamics, conformity, homogenisation and hybrids

### 2.1 Chuang et al. 2024 [V]
Chuang, Y.-S., Goyal, A., Harlalka, N., Suresh, S., Hawkins, R., Yang, S., Shah, D., Hu, J., Rogers, T.T. (2024). *Simulating Opinion Dynamics with Networks of LLM-based Agents.* Findings of NAACL 2024, pp. 3326-3346 (venue from search result); arXiv:2311.09618. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09618
Claim: LLM agents have a strong inherent bias toward accurate information, so simulated populations converge to the scientific consensus; this limits their use for studying resistance to consensus. Inducing confirmation bias through prompts produced opinion fragmentation resembling classical ABM predictions.
**Implication:** this is the central justification for doing opinion updates in Python. Left to the LLM, our residents would drift toward whatever the closed corpus "says" and toward consensus. Our code-owned Deffuant/Baumann/Chacoma-Zanette rules prevent that by construction. Report this explicitly. (Caveat: they used prompt-induced confirmation bias; we do not need to.)

### 2.2 Conformity, sycophancy and homogenisation in multi-agent LLM systems
- Sharma, M. et al. (2024). *Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models.* ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.13548. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548 [V] State-of-the-art assistants consistently show sycophancy, likely driven in part by human preference judgments. **Implication:** instruction-tuned Apertus will tend to agree with whichever neighbour just spoke; this is why the LLM should voice, not decide.
- *When Your AI Agent Succumbs to Peer-Pressure: Studying Opinion-Change Dynamics of LLMs.* arXiv:2510.19107. https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.19107 [V] Opinion change follows a sigmoid curve in the fraction of dissenting peers, with model-dependent thresholds (one model needs over 70% peer disagreement to flip, another reacts to minority dissent) and a persuasion asymmetry between positive-to-negative and negative-to-positive change. **Implication:** if we ever let the LLM judge "am I persuaded?", expect threshold behaviour rather than gradual drift; this is a point in favour of Deffuant-style continuous updates, and a candidate check: compare our code's drift to an LLM-judged stance on a handful of exchanges.
- Han, C. et al. (2026). *Conformity Dynamics in LLM Multi-Agent Systems: The Roles of Topology and Self-Social Weighting.* arXiv:2601.05606. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05606 [V] More connectivity speeds convergence but raises the risk of "wrong-but-sure cascades". **Implication:** keep the interaction graph sparse and log it; full mixing in a 10-agent town is the worst case for cascades.
- Kasprova et al. (2026). *Too Polite to Disagree: Understanding Sycophancy Propagation in Multi-Agent Systems.* arXiv:2604.02668, SIGDIAL 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02668 [V] Telling agents about peers' sycophancy tendencies reduces error cascades and improved final discussion accuracy by an absolute 10.5% (task is factual, not political). **Implication:** limited transfer to opinion tasks, but supports giving each resident a code-owned "stubbornness" parameter.
- *The Deliberative Illusion: Diagnosing Factual Attrition and Stance Homogenization in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation.* arXiv:2606.03032. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03032 [V] Discussion can erase up to 72% of issue-critical facts while stances homogenise (DelibTrace framework). **Implication:** re-inject corpus facts each round via retrieval rather than relying on dialogue memory, and measure fact survival (a cheap metric: proportion of key corpus facts mentioned per round).
- Sela, A. (2026). *Preserving Disagreement: Architectural Heterogeneity and Coherence Validation in Multi-Agent Policy Simulation.* arXiv:2604.26561. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26561 [V] Using different 7-9B models per agent reduced uniform agreement (for example 70.9% to 46.1% on one policy scenario, p < 0.001); coherence validation had mixed effects; small models responded to counterarguments in a "binary rather than graded" way. **Implication:** we run one model (Apertus) so cannot copy model heterogeneity; we can get partial heterogeneity from role-specific retrieval and code-owned stances. The "binary" finding for small models is a reason to avoid asking Apertus-8B for graded stance numbers.

### 2.3 Dynamics of LLM opinions and hybrid LLM-plus-equation designs
- Probine, C. et al. (2026). *Characterizing Opinion Evolution of Networked LLMs.* arXiv:2606.18276. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18276 [V] Plain averaging models fail to describe LLM opinion dynamics; adding "an innate opinion toward which agents regress" cut prediction error by up to 88% in some cases, consistently across model families, topics and networks. **Implication:** if any LLM-derived stance enters our loop, it behaves like a pull toward a model-specific prior. Our design (code-owned stance seeded from persona) avoids that; an optional "anchor" term in the Python update would be the principled place to represent persona stubbornness.
- Pokharel, A., Dantu, R. (2026). *Hidden Anchors in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation.* arXiv:2606.19494. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19494 [V] Models deliberation as a dynamical system with persistent internal "anchors" per agent, extractable from deliberation traces and validated on held-out scenarios, across three open-source model families. **Implication:** same message as Probine: unconstrained LLM agents carry hidden anchors; making ours explicit and in code is the cleaner design.
- Banerjee, T., Moghaddas, N. (2026). *Coherence-Driven Belief Formation and Population Dynamics of Contagion in LLM Agents.* arXiv:2610.02654 (NeurIPS 2026 workshop). https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02654 [V] Belief adoption by LLM agents follows a sigmoid ("complex contagion"), depends on plausibility, source reliability and disposition, and shows hysteresis once consensus forms. **Implication:** matches the Chacoma-Zanette keep/compromise/adopt structure in spirit; supports adding source reliability (e.g. "official corpus" vs "neighbour hearsay") as a weight on adoption.
- Yao, J. et al. (2025). *Social opinions prediction utilizes fusing dynamics equation with LLM-based agents (FDE-LLM).* Scientific Reports; arXiv:2409.08717. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08717 [V] Opinion leaders are LLM role-players whose opinion shifts are constrained by cellular automata; followers follow a CA-SIR dynamics; reports outperforming pure ABM and pure LLM baselines on four Weibo datasets. **Implication:** the closest published hybrid to GemeindeSim's design (LLM voice plus equation-constrained opinion). Cite as precedent; their validation is on social-media sentiment time series, not votes.
- Berjawi, O. et al. (2026). *Digital Twins for Opinion Dynamics: A Generative LLM Framework for Social Networks.* arXiv:2609.19913. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19913 [V] Mistral-7B agents on a cloned Twitter network; reports MAE of 0.150 (COVID-19) and 0.121 (2020 US election) and over 50% improvement over traditional baselines; ablation found agent characteristics (persona, emotion, stubbornness) the most important factor. **Implication:** supports enriching personas with stubbornness and emotion fields; again validation is on Twitter data, not ballots.
- Reji, D.J. (2026). *LLM-Agent-based Social Simulation for Attitude Diffusion.* arXiv:2604.03898. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03898 [V] Open-source framework, 100 agents over a 15-day simulation of a real event, positioned as theory-testing rather than prediction. **Implication:** a comparable-scale precedent and a model for framing.
- He, X. et al. (2026). *LLM Agents Make Collective Belief Dynamics Programmable.* arXiv:2605.19915. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19915 [V] Simulations show coordinated AI agents can induce belief shifts that stabilise within a few rounds. **Implication:** a safety note for the "report must not recommend how to vote" requirement: a simulation that shows persuasion trajectories could be misused to optimise persuasion; keep the "no recommendations" guard and avoid exposing "most persuasive message" outputs.

### 2.4 Classical models we rely on [V]
- Chacoma, A., Zanette, D.H. (2015). *Opinion Formation by Social Influence: From Experiments to Modeling.* PLoS ONE 10(10):e0140406. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0140406 The experiment had 85 undergraduates answer 20 quantitative questions twice (before and after seeing reference answers); subjects kept their opinion unchanged in roughly 60% of cases. **Implication and caveat:** this empirical keep/compromise/adopt behaviour was measured on numeric estimation questions with reference answers, not on value-laden political stances. Applying it to a vote is an extrapolation; state it as a modelling assumption, and consider making the keep probability a documented parameter with a sensitivity sweep.
- Peralta, A.F., Kertész, J., Iñiguez, G. (2022). *Opinion dynamics in social networks: From models to data.* arXiv:2201.01322. https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01322 Review of idealised opinion-dynamics models and attempts to validate them with observational data and experiments. **Implication:** a general citation for the validation gap; use it to explain why we expose parameters rather than claim calibrated values.
- Baumann, F., Lorenz-Spreen, P., Sokolov, I.M., Starnini, M. (2020). *Modeling Echo Chambers and Polarization Dynamics in Social Networks.* Phys. Rev. Lett. 124:048301. https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.124.048301 The transition between consensus and polarised states is governed by social-influence strength and topic controversialness. **Implication:** supports using a per-issue controversy parameter; for Swiss local votes it should be set per measure (e.g. from the corpus or from past vote margins), not a global constant.
- Deffuant et al. (2000) bounded confidence: [S] I did not open the original paper; cite from the Chacoma/Peralta literature or verify before use.

## 3. Political and voting behaviour of LLMs, Swiss focus, and Apertus' public record

### 3.1 Swiss direct democracy and LLMs
- **Barmettler, J.P. (2026).** *Progressive in Principle, Centrist in Practice: LLM Political Bias Is Instrument-Dependent* (v1 title: *The Invisible Coalition Partner: How LLMs Vote When Democracy Gets Concrete*). arXiv:2606.00048. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00048 [V; details from the v3 HTML]. Compares an abstract smartvote questionnaire (66 LLMs) with concrete voting on 48 Swiss federal Volksabstimmungen (March 2021 to March 2026) using official booklets, for 9 flagship models (GPT-5.4, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, DeepSeek V3.2, Llama 4 Maverick, Grok 4.20, Mistral Large 2512, Qwen 3.5 Plus, Command A) in German, French, Italian and Romansh, under three information conditions. Findings: the left-peaked agreement gradient on smartvote (mean rho = -0.77) became center-peaked on referenda (mean rho = +0.34; Wilcoxon p = 0.008); alignment with popular-vote outcomes ranged from 97.9% (GPT-5.4) to 60.4% (Grok); cross-language consistency ranged from 97.9% (GPT-5.4) to 50.0% (Mistral), with Mistral's yes-rate swinging from 17% in German to 82% in Romansh; the Röstigraben test had about 55% power; smartvote was administered only in German; only 8 usable models for the referendum analysis. Apertus is not among the models (the paper does not mention it).
  **Implication for GemeindeSim (high value):** (i) this is the only direct study of LLMs on Swiss referendums I found, and it gives us a protocol to copy: feed the official Abstimmungsbüchlein / Gemeinde-Botschaft text, run per language, and report cross-language consistency; (ii) cross-language inconsistency can be large and unrelated to real regional voting patterns, so our DE and FR runs must be compared before we claim bilingual parity; (iii) abstract political leaning ("left" field in personas) predicts concrete vote behaviour poorly, so a persona's "political leaning" label should not be trusted to drive stance on a specific municipal measure; (iv) the heavy centrist/"accept official proposal" tendency on concrete votes suggests a built-in bias toward the status quo or authority framing; (v) note the agreement with outcomes was measured with national-level booklets and frontier models, not 8B/70B open models in agent loops.
- **Reveilhac, M., Morselli, D.** *ChatGPT as a voting application in direct democracy.* Swiss Political Science Review 31(3). https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spsr.12650 [S: Wiley returned HTTP 403; abstract read in a search result]. Simulated Swiss voter profiles (neutral, centrist, left, right, progressist, conservative) on four federal objects, 100 answers per question at fixed temperature; decisions varied by model version (GPT-3.5 vs GPT-4) and prompt language (French vs German); no consistent left-progressive pattern. **Implication:** the same measure can flip between DE and FR prompts; our DE/FR toggle is a live validity risk, and the 100-samples-at-fixed-temperature design is a cheap template for our consistency check.
- Yuan, Y., Salamanca, L., Schlosser, S., Brandenberger, L. (2026). *Graph-Augmented LLMs for Swiss MP Ideology Prediction.* arXiv:2605.04643 (accepted at SwissText 26). https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04643 [V] A political-knowledge-graph RAG (PG-RAG) beats text-only baselines on Swiss parliamentary stance estimation. **Implication:** structured relational context (party, committee, past votes) helps; for municipal sims, adding a small structured "who belongs to which association/party" table to the corpus is a lightweight analogue.
- *Can LLMs Forecast Swiss Referendum Outcomes?* ETH Zurich Distributed Computing project call. https://tik-db.ee.ethz.ch/file/2ee9558e5f8cbf4a6c5103b814a809a8/DISCO_distLLMs_referendum.pdf [V as to content] I extracted the PDF text: it is a student project announcement with no results. Not usable as evidence; listed so nobody cites it as a finding.

### 3.2 Non-US silicon sampling and voting prediction
- von der Heyde, L., Haensch, A.-C., Wenz, A., Ma, B. (2024). *United in Diversity? Contextual Biases in LLM-Based Predictions of the 2024 European Parliament Elections.* arXiv:2409.09045. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09045 [V] On about 26,000 European voters, LLM predictions "largely fail", with accuracy unequally distributed across national and linguistic contexts. **Implication:** do not claim vote prediction; the evidence outside the US is negative.
- Ma, B. et al. (2025). *Algorithmic Fidelity of Large Language Models in Generating Synthetic German Public Opinions: A Case Study.* ACL 2025, arXiv:2412.13169. https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13169 [V] Models matched Greens and Left supporters better than AfD supporters; Llama did best at representing subpopulations when within-group opinion diversity was low; prompt variables strongly affect predictions. **Implication:** political asymmetry is the norm; our right-of-centre residents (SVP-like personas) are likely to be portrayed less faithfully than left-of-centre ones. Include them anyway and audit separately.
- Gudiño, J.F., Grandi, U., Hidalgo, C. (2024). *Large language models (LLMs) as agents for augmented democracy.* Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A 382:20240100. [S] Search results: Brazil 2022 case study, LLM-augmented samples gave better aggregate estimates than the non-augmented sample alone; no Swiss case. Not opened. Mentioned only as a "digital twin voter" precedent.

### 3.3 Political bias in multilingual models
- Helwe, C., Balalau, O., Ceolin, D. (2025). *Navigating the Political Compass: Evaluating Multilingual LLMs across Languages and Nationalities.* Findings of ACL 2025. https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.883/ [V] 15 multilingual models; prompt language significantly changes the displayed ideology; smaller models show more stable ideology. **Implication:** supports an explicit DE-vs-FR run of each scenario; the "smaller is more stable" result mildly favours using the 8B for stance-sensitive prompts, but this is one finding, not a rule.
- Liu, Y., Kaneko, M., Chu, C. (2025). *On the Alignment of Large Language Models with Global Human Opinion.* arXiv:2509.01418. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01418 [V] Prompting in a country's native language substantially improves alignment compared to existing steering techniques; alignment is good for only a handful of countries. **Implication:** keep residents' prompts in their own language (a FR-speaking resident in French) rather than prompting everything in English or translating.

### 3.4 What is actually public about Apertus
Sources: Apertus technical report arXiv:2509.14233 (abstract page and a local text extraction of the PDF), Hugging Face cards for swiss-ai/Apertus-v1.5-8B and -70B, apertus-ai.org release article, NLP-Lab blog posts of 2026-07-29 and 2026-10-01, a Liip blog post, and a KU Leuven summer-school blog post. All [V] unless noted.

**Release and sizes.** Apertus 1.5 released 24 July 2026 (apertus-ai.org article). 8B and 70B; continued pretraining adds 4T tokens (8B) and 2T tokens (70B) of text and multimodal data; context 262,144 tokens, four times Apertus 1.0 (65,536). Image input, experimental audio input; output is text only. License Apache 2.0, with an Acceptable Use Policy gate on the 8B card.
**Languages.** Apertus 1.0 report: 15T tokens, over 1,800 languages, about 40% non-English data (abstract); post-training included Swiss-German dialect instructions (6K examples), 46,923 Romansh instruction examples, and 226 constitutional-alignment examples following the Swiss AI Charter (technical report text). The 1.5 pages say "massively multilingual" and cite FineWeb-2's 1,811 languages, but state no per-language performance or Swiss-German evaluation. The Apertus blog post says breadth "does not guarantee equal performance". I found no public Swiss-German (spoken dialect) evaluation for 1.5. The 1.0 recommended system prompt tells the model to "use Swiss High German (no ss-sharp s)" for German output.
**Thinking mode and tool calling.** Optional thinking mode via `enable_thinking=True`; the 70B card warns reasoning can be "several times longer than the visible answer" so budget `max_new_tokens`. Tool calling is supported (vLLM flags `--enable-auto-tool-choice --tool-call-parser apertus` on the 8B card) but "the current official setup does not support tool calling in thinking mode" (NLP-Lab and HF cards). NLP-Lab benchmark post: thinking improves math (MATH-500 71%, MGSM 79.1%) but hurts instruction following (IFEval 85.8 to 72.1) and is neutral-to-negative on factual recall; multilingual HellaSwag regressed by 8.3 points; "evaluation tooling has a measurable effect on results" exceeding ten points. **Implication:** for JSON-producing agent turns, keep thinking off; the IFEval drop is the relevant number.
**Sampling.** Neither HF card gives recommended temperature or top_p. (Our all-temperature-0 setup is therefore not contradicted by the vendor, but it is also not endorsed.)
**Safety, charter, neutrality.** Alignment includes a separate pipeline for controversial topics using a "Swiss AI Charter" (11 articles; neutrality, consensus-building, federalism, multilingualism, cultural diversity), applied via an LLM judge (Qwen3-32B) prompt; a survey of Swiss residents gave an average 97.3% agreement with the charter's principles (report text). The recommended system prompt includes "Present information neutrally, separating facts from advocacy" and "Acknowledge multiple viewpoints fairly". The report's Safety Advisory Statement says the models may hallucinate, degenerate, generate toxic output and "should only be deployed after extensive use-case alignment and additional testing." The HF cards say outputs "may not always be factually accurate, logically consistent, or free from biases".
**Independent probe (not peer reviewed).** Peter, O. (10 Feb 2026), KU Leuven AI Summer School blog, *Will Swiss AI Save Swiss Democracy?* https://www.law.kuleuven.be/ai-summer-school/blogpost/Blogposts/will-swiss-ai-save-swiss-democracy-an-investigation-of-apertus-perception-of-democracy : on World Values Survey democracy items, Apertus aligned better with Swiss citizens after alignment training but stayed "closer to a US representation of democracy than a Swiss one"; alignment caused "mode collapse" on income-equality questions; the author notes small scale, brittle outputs and prompt/order sensitivity. This is about Apertus 1.0, not 1.5. **Implication:** do not claim the Swiss Charter makes Apertus a neutral Swiss voter; mode collapse under alignment is the same flattening risk as in 4.3.
**Memorisation.** Goldfish loss is used in pretraining to suppress verbatim recall (0.3T of 15T tokens were masked); the report notes a failure mode: hashing of a 50-token context is fragile to near-duplicates, and the most-memorised sequences were canonical works (poems, plays, the US Constitution, the Bible). **Implication:** low risk of verbatim leakage of local documents; also means do not expect the model to reproduce municipal documents verbatim from memory, so the closed corpus is needed.
**Data compliance.** Training used openly available data with robots.txt opt-outs respected retroactively (applying January 2025 preferences to older crawls), and filtering of non-permissive, toxic and personal data (abstract and report text).
**Swiss knowledge.** SwitzerlandQA (custom benchmark, Geography, History, Social Life, Political Life, Insurance; in en/de/fr/it/rm). My text extraction of the report table gives roughly 62.1 (8B) and 60.2 (70B) for Apertus 1.0 on SwitzerlandQA, but the table layout was ambiguous in extraction; treat as approximate and check the PDF before quoting. The report itself warns coverage across cantons is uneven and the Romansh subset has higher translation risk. **Implication:** do not assume local-knowledge competence at municipal level; grounding in a closed corpus is justified.
**Knowledge cutoff.** The 1.0 recommended system prompt states a March 2024 cutoff. I did not find a stated cutoff for 1.5.
**What I could not find:** an Apertus-specific evaluation on political questions in DE/FR/IT; tool-calling accuracy numbers; Swiss-German evaluation; recommended sampling parameters; the Apertus 1.5 technical report (the release article said "in the coming weeks"; I did not check whether it has since appeared).

## 4. Evaluating agent simulations: distribution, diversity, consistency, persona robustness, judges

### 4.1 Distributional alignment and benchmarks
- Hu, T., Baumann, J., Lupo, L., Collier, N., Hovy, D., Röttger, P. (2026). *SimBench: Benchmarking the Ability of Large Language Models to Simulate Human Behaviors.* ICLR 2026, arXiv:2510.17516. https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17516 [V] 20 unified datasets; best model scored 40.80/100 ("meaningful but modest"); instruction tuning helps on consensus questions and hurts on high-diversity ones; models struggle with specific demographic groups; simulation ability correlates with MMLU-Pro (r = 0.939). **Implication:** supports using the larger Apertus (70B) for persona-bearing utterances if budget allows, and flags that alignment tuning (Apertus has a charter-driven pipeline) can cost diversity. Candidate: evaluate our persona prompts on a few SimBench-style items.
- Hu, T., Collier, N. (2024). *Quantifying the Persona Effect in LLM Simulations.* ACL 2024, arXiv:2402.10811. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10811 [V] Persona variables explain under 10% of annotation variance in existing subjective datasets; persona prompting gives modest but significant gains, largest when annotators disagree mildly; a 70B model with personas reached 81% of the variance a linear regression trained on ground truth achieved. **Implication:** do not expect persona fields to produce large behavioural differences; a big difference between residents will mostly come from code-owned stance and role-specific information, not the persona text.
- Liu et al. 2025 (3.3) and Santurkar et al. (1.4) supply the Wasserstein/distribution framing; I did not open the survey-fine-tuning papers surfaced by search (arXiv:2502.16761, 2507.00439) so they are excluded.

### 4.2 Population-level diversity and persona collapse
- Xiao, Y. et al. (2026). *The Chameleon's Limit: Investigating Persona Collapse and Homogenization in Large Language Models.* arXiv:2604.24698. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24698 [V] Introduces Coverage, Uniformity and Complexity metrics for populations of persona agents across 10 LLMs; counterintuitively, models with the highest per-persona fidelity produced the most stereotyped populations; toolkit released. **Implication:** adopt a coverage/uniformity measure on residents' utterances and stances; do not use per-persona "does this sound like a shopkeeper" as the only quality check.
- Ozkan, G. (2026). *Distribution-First Population Simulation: Collapse, Calibration, and Recall in Non-WEIRD LLM Persona Modeling.* arXiv:2607.18310. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18310 [V] With 2,414 World Values Survey respondents, independent LLM agents collapse onto modal answers (entropy 1.46 to 0.77; 85% of units collapsed); Verbalized Sampling corrected under-dispersion by 7-10 points but overshot into over-dispersion; survey answers transfer poorly to agentic behaviour (personas drift toward cost-minimising defaults regardless of income). **Implication:** supports tracking entropy/concentration of residents' stances; the cost-minimising-default finding is directly relevant to budget votes (all residents may drift to "reduce cost").
- Bui, N. et al. (2025). *Mixture-of-Personas Language Models for Population Simulation.* arXiv:2504.05019. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05019 [V] Samples persona-plus-exemplar pairs from learned mixture weights to increase diversity without fine-tuning. **Implication:** a cheap variation: give each resident one randomly chosen exemplar utterance (written by us in DE or FR) in the prompt, rather than only a persona sentence.
- Li, A., Chen, H., Namkoong, H., Peng, T. (2025). *LLM Generated Persona is a Promise with a Catch.* arXiv:2503.16527. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16527 [V] LLM-generated personas introduce systematic biases that cause significant deviations from real outcomes (election forecasting and opinion surveys). Search snippets add that generated personas skew progressive and positive; I did not verify that detail on the page. **Implication:** write the 5-25 personas by hand (or from Swiss statistics) instead of asking Apertus to invent them.
- Temperature: Peeperkorn, M. et al. (2024). *Is Temperature the Creativity Parameter of Large Language Models?* ICCC 2024, arXiv:2405.00492. https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00492 [S, search result only] Temperature is weakly correlated with novelty and moderately with incoherence. **Implication (weak, unverified page):** raising temperature is a poor way to get diverse residents; prefer persona/exemplar and code-owned stance diversity. Also see 4.4 on non-determinism at temperature 0.
- Diversity metrics: Zhu, Y. et al. (2018). *Texygen: A Benchmarking Platform for Text Generation Models.* arXiv:1802.01886 [S] (introduces Self-BLEU; page not opened but the search result is explicit); Li, J., Galley, M., Brockett, C., Gao, J., Dolan, B. (2016). *A Diversity-Promoting Objective Function for Neural Conversation Models.* NAACL 2016, arXiv:1510.03055 [V] (the distinct-n measure is commonly credited to this paper; the abstract I read does not itself mention distinct-n, so verify before attributing). **Implication:** compute Self-BLEU or distinct-2 across residents' utterances per round as a homogenisation dashboard metric (a few lines of Python).

### 4.3 Persona-prompting robustness
- Morocho, et al. (2026). *Assessing the Reliability of Persona-Conditioned LLMs as Synthetic Survey Respondents.* arXiv:2602.18462. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18462 [V] Two open-weight chat models, over 70,000 World Values Survey responses: persona prompting "does not yield a clear aggregate improvement" and often degrades alignment; error is redistributed to certain items and underrepresented subgroups. **Implication:** a persona block can hurt; run a "no persona" ablation on the stance-relevant utterances. (First-author initial not on the page I read.)
- de Araujo, P.H.L., Röttger, P., Hovy, D., Roth, B. (2025). *Principled Personas: Defining and Measuring the Intended Effects of Persona Prompting on Task Performance.* EMNLP 2025, arXiv:2508.19764. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19764 [V] 9 LLMs, 27 tasks; models are vulnerable to irrelevant persona details (drops of almost 30 percentage points); mitigations worked only for the largest models. **Implication:** MBTI and similar irrelevant fields can degrade behaviour and factual grounding on 8B; test dropping MBTI.
- Ye et al. 2026 (TRAILS) and Bisbee et al. 2024 (see 1.5, 1.9) give the perturbation-audit template.
- Persona as double-edged sword (Kim et al., arXiv:2408.08631) surfaced in search; [S] not opened, not relied on.

### 4.4 Consistency and reproducibility
- Atil, B. et al. (2024). *Non-Determinism of "Deterministic" LLM Settings.* arXiv:2408.04667. https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667 [V] Even at temperature 0, accuracy varied up to 15% across runs for five LLMs on eight tasks, with best-to-worst gaps up to 70%; the authors link it to infrastructure effects. **Implication:** "temperature 0" does not mean reproducible. Record seed, model id, endpoint and timestamp per run, and report results over several runs; do not claim determinism in the paper. (If the Apertus endpoint supports a seed parameter, use it; I did not verify that it does.)

### 4.5 LLM-as-judge pitfalls
- Zheng, L. et al. (2023). *Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena.* NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks, arXiv:2306.05685. https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685 [V] Identifies position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases; strong judges exceeded 80% agreement with human preferences. **Implication:** if we use an LLM to rate "believability" or "report quality", swap the order, avoid scoring Apertus with Apertus, and calibrate on 20-30 human-rated items. Prefer programmatic checks (numeral, citation, language) wherever possible.
- Larooij and Törnberg (1.9) note that subjective believability judgement dominates validation in this field; our own eval should not rest on it.

## 5. Structured output reliability and RAG grounding

### 5.1 JSON and constrained decoding
- Geng, S. et al. (2025). *JSONSchemaBench: A Rigorous Benchmark of Structured Outputs for Language Models.* arXiv:2501.10868. https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10868 [V] 10,000 real-world schemas, six constrained-decoding frameworks. A search summary reports coverage dropping from 86% on simple schemas to 3% on the most complex ones; I did not verify that number on the page. **Implication:** keep our action schemas flat and small; test each schema against the actual serving stack rather than assuming support.
- Chavan, A. (2026). *Constrained Decoding Eliminates Structural Failures in Small LLMs but Reveals a Scale-Dependent Semantic Gap.* arXiv:2609.23742. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23742 [V] On 0.6B-4B models across 14 tasks, constrained decoding took schema validity from 78.6-92.9% to 100% but did not fix "instruction-semantic failures"; "schema conformance is necessary but not sufficient". **Implication:** (models are smaller than Apertus 8B) use grammar/JSON-schema constrained decoding if the endpoint supports it, and still validate semantic constraints in Python (allowed action names, targets that exist, numerals in corpus).
- Tam, Z.R. et al. (2024). *Let Me Speak Freely? A Study on the Impact of Format Restrictions on Performance of Large Language Models.* EMNLP 2024 Industry Track, arXiv:2408.02442. https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442 [V] Stricter format constraints generally degrade reasoning performance. **Implication:** do not ask the same call to both reason and emit strict JSON; use free text for the utterance and a second small structured call (or code parsing) for the action.

### 5.2 Grounding, attribution, faithfulness
- Gao, T., Yen, H., Yu, J., Chen, D. (2023). *Enabling Large Language Models to Generate Text with Citations (ALCE).* EMNLP 2023, arXiv:2305.14627. https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14627 [V] Benchmark with fluency, correctness and citation-quality metrics; on ELI5 even the best models lacked complete citation support about 50% of the time. **Implication:** expect roughly one in two cited claims to be insufficiently supported even with good models; build a post-hoc check that each cited passage id exists and shares key terms or numerals with the claim.
- Es, S., James, J., Espinosa-Anke, L., Schockaert, S. (2023, rev. 2025). *Ragas: Automated Evaluation of Retrieval Augmented Generation.* arXiv:2309.15217. https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217 [V] Reference-free metrics for retrieval quality, faithful use of context and generation quality. **Implication:** log context relevance (did retrieval return the passage that contains the answer?) separately from faithfulness (did the claim follow from the retrieved passage?); our "numerals not in the corpus are stripped" rule is a simple, strict special case of faithfulness. Note that Ragas uses an LLM as judge, so see 4.5.
- Numerals specifically: a search summary says LLMs "frequently suffer from hallucinations such as misquoting figures or altering numerical values" in data-intensive RAG, but I did not open that paper; our own metric (fraction of generated numerals that were stripped, per run) will give direct evidence.

### 5.3 Cross-lingual retrieval for DE/FR
- Park, J., Lee, H. (2025). *Investigating Language Preference of Multilingual RAG Systems.* Findings of ACL 2025, arXiv:2502.11175. https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11175 [V] Retrievers prefer high-resource and query languages, which does not consistently improve generation; generators prefer the query language or Latin scripts, producing inconsistent outputs across conflicting multilingual sources. **Implication:** if the corpus is mixed DE/FR, an FR query may pull FR documents and a DE query DE documents, so the two language runs may see different facts; for fair DE/FR comparison use a parallel corpus (same content in both languages) or fix the retrieved passage set across languages.
- Zhang, X. et al. (2023). *MIRACL: A Multilingual Retrieval Dataset Covering 18 Diverse Languages.* TACL 11. https://aclanthology.org/2023.tacl-1.63/ [V as to dataset; the BM25-vs-dense statement was not on the page I read]. A search snippet claims that mDPR does not substantially outperform BM25 on average, that BM25 is weaker in French, and that a BM25-mDPR hybrid is a strong zero-shot baseline [S]. **Implication (tentative):** lexical retrieval over French text should be tested explicitly; add light stemming/accent normalisation, and consider a hybrid with a multilingual embedding model if recall on a hand-labelled set is poor.
- Swiss legal multilingual corpora exist (Swiss-Judgment-Prediction, SwiLTra-Bench, SMILED, Swiss legal summarisation), seen in search results [S]. I opened one: Stern, R. et al. (2025). *From Citations to Criticality: Predicting Legal Decision Influence in the Multilingual Swiss Jurisprudence.* ACL 2025, arXiv:2410.13460 [V]: smaller fine-tuned models beat larger zero-shot LLMs on this Swiss task. **Implication:** limited; shows Swiss multilingual NLP resources are available if we need German/French test text, and that domain-specific tuning can beat general LLMs.

## 6. Cost and scalability for small civic simulations

- Yu, Y. et al. (2024). *Affordable Generative Agents.* arXiv:2402.02053. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02053 [V] Replaces repeated LLM calls with learned policies for agent-environment interaction and compresses inter-agent dialogue; notes agents can generate only finite behaviours in fixed environments. **Implication:** at 5-25 agents we do not need this; the useful ideas are (a) compress dialogue history into a relationship/opinion summary instead of replaying it and (b) skip LLM calls on rounds where code decides an agent does nothing.
- OASIS and AgentSociety (1.9) show that 10^4-10^6 agents are feasible with engineering; our regime is not scale-limited, it is call-latency- and rate-limit-limited on a hosted endpoint. **Implication:** concurrent (async) per-round calls, a per-run call budget, and caching of retrieval results are the proportionate measures. I found no paper specifically about 5-25-agent civic sims, and I did not verify claims in search summaries about batching or plan-caching papers (arXiv:2509.23586, 2506.14852, 2509.02121), so they are not relied on.
- "Lazy"/stateless agent designs: I found no verified paper using that term in this context; omitted.

## 7. Concrete improvements the literature suggests

Ranked by value for a two-week hackathon and feasibility. Effort: S = hours, M = 1-2 days, L = 3+ days.

1. **Add an explicit per-resident stance variable (code-owned) and make it the object of all dynamics and metrics. [High value, S-M]**
   Seed it from role/interest and the specific measure (not from the "political leaning" label). Let the LLM voice the stance, never set it. Supported by: Chuang 2024 (LLM truth/consensus bias), Probine 2026 and Pokharel 2026 (hidden anchors in LLM-only dynamics), Barmettler 2026 (abstract leaning predicts concrete votes poorly), Larooij and Törnberg 2025 (mechanism transparency), S3 and FDE-LLM (explicit attitude variables). Also lets us plot stance trajectories and report a conformity measure.

2. **Population-level diversity and homogenisation metrics on the dashboard. [High, S]**
   Per round: stance entropy/concentration, spread (std), Self-BLEU or distinct-2 on utterances, fraction of key corpus facts still mentioned (fact survival). Flag a "collapse" warning. Supported by: Ozkan 2026, Xiao 2026 (Chameleon's Limit), Bisbee 2024, Zhu 2018, Li 2016, Deliberative Illusion 2026.

3. **Perturbation and language audit (a TRAILS-lite). [High, M]**
   For each shipped scenario run: persona paraphrase, persona with MBTI removed, no persona, DE vs FR prompt language, shuffled speaking order, 3-5 seeds. Report stance spread and the DE-FR gap. Supported by: Ye 2026, Barmettler 2026 (cross-language consistency), Reveilhac and Morselli, Morocho 2026, de Araujo 2025, Bisbee 2024, Atil 2024. This also converts the temperature-0 gap into a documented result.

4. **Citation and numeral faithfulness metrics. [High, S-M]**
   Log per run: share of generated numerals stripped, share of cited passage ids that exist, term/numeral overlap between claim and cited passage, and retrieval hit rate on a small hand-labelled set (20-30 questions, DE and FR). Supported by: ALCE (about half of cited outputs incompletely supported even for top models), Ragas, Deliberative Illusion.

5. **Richer personas, handwritten, with a few exemplar utterances. [Medium-high, M]**
   5-10 lines of life situation and values, plus one example sentence in the resident's language; drop MBTI unless the audit (item 3) shows it helps; write the personas by hand or from Swiss statistics. Supported by: Park 2024 (grounding beats demographics), Bui 2025 (exemplars), Li 2025 (LLM-generated personas biased), Hu and Collier 2024 (persona alone explains little), de Araujo 2025 (irrelevant persona details hurt).

6. **Structured-output hardening. [Medium, S]**
   Thinking off for agent turns (Apertus IFEval drop 85.8 to 72.1 with thinking); constrained decoding or JSON-schema mode if the endpoint supports it; Python-side semantic validation (known targets, allowed actions); split "speak" (free text) from "act" (small JSON). Supported by: Chavan 2026, Geng 2025, Tam 2024, NLP-Lab benchmark post.

7. **A small backtest protocol against real Swiss outcomes, even if only qualitative. [Medium, M-L]**
   Copy Barmettler's design: official voting booklet text as the corpus, 3-5 past Swiss measures with known results (and, if available, municipality or canton level results; I saw a municipality-level 1866-2023 dataset title in a search listing but did not open it), compare direction of the sim's yes/no balance and its DE/FR difference with the real Röstigraben pattern. Present as exploratory with the explicit caveat that Europe/Swiss prediction evidence is negative (von der Heyde 2024). Supported by: Barmettler 2026, Hullman 2026, Li and Ji 2026, Hewitt 2026 (compare conditions, not absolute shares).

8. **Honest framing and safeguards in the report. [Medium, S]**
   Describe the tool as a pilot/what-if explorer; show uncertainty across seeds; state fictional archetypes (Wang 2025); no "most persuasive message" output (He 2026 on programmable belief shifts); keep the no-voting-recommendation rule and test it with adversarial prompts. Supported by: Anthis 2025, Larooij and Törnberg 2025, Li and Tao 2026, Hullman 2026, Apertus Charter's neutrality principles (but see Peter 2026 for the limits of assuming the model is neutral).

9. **Parallel-corpus and retrieval check for DE/FR fairness. [Medium, S-M]**
   Fix the retrieved passages across DE and FR runs (or use a parallel corpus), add accent/stem normalisation, test a lexical + embedding hybrid if recall is poor. Supported by: Park and Lee 2025, MIRACL (tentative [S]).

10. **Sensitivity sweep for the opinion-dynamics parameters. [Medium, S]**
    Chacoma-Zanette's keep/compromise/adopt rates come from 85 students on numeric estimates; sweep keep/compromise/adopt, Deffuant confidence bound and Baumann controversy and show how conclusions change. Supported by: Chacoma and Zanette 2015, Peralta 2022, Baumann 2020, Coherence-Driven Belief Formation 2026 (sigmoid and hysteresis).

11. **Cheap cost controls. [Low, S]**
    Async calls per round, history compression to summaries, skip LLM calls for idle agents. Supported by: Affordable Generative Agents; not a bottleneck at 5-25 agents.

12. **Optional heterogeneity beyond persona text. [Low-medium, L]**
    Different model sizes for different residents (Apertus 8B for some, 70B for others) as a poor-man's version of architectural heterogeneity. Supported by: Sela 2026 (a different setup: different 7-9B model families; no evidence about same-family size mixes). Treat as speculative.
